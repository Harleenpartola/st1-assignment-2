Before using AI, I built a simple Python prototype that stored two appointments and printed them to the screen. I then enhanced it using lists, dictionaries, and functions, which helped me understand how appointment data can be structured and displayed. My human‑written version worked, but it had several limitations, such as no validation, no conflict checking, and no handling of unusual input.

AI helped me understand the purpose of each part of my code and pointed out issues I had not considered, such as preventing blank names or duplicate bookings. It also suggested improvements without rewriting the entire program, which allowed me to stay in control of the design. The AI did make assumptions, such as adding validation rules or suggesting features that were not required for Stage 1, so I had to decide which suggestions were appropriate.

To verify the AI output, I tested normal appointments, blank names, duplicate times, and None values. This helped me confirm which improvements were useful and which were unnecessary. Even with AI assistance, the engineering work remained my responsibility: defining requirements, testing behaviour, deciding what features were in scope, and ensuring the program met the SmartCare brief.
